Executive Summary

Executive Summary

GeoSuite (“the System”) is a full-stack geospatial platform developed to solve a practical, growing problem in land administration and surveying: paper cadastral plans remain trapped on paper. The System reads scanned survey plans — from age‑hardened georeferenced drawings to modern field captures — and automatically digitises them into structured, machine‑readable products: missing beacons, parcel boundaries, plan labels and coordinate estimates.

Under the hood, a purpose‑built computer-vision engine (KLISS CV Engine v0.1.0) processes plan imagery through a shared convolutional backbone and four specialist heads: a Beacon Detector, a Boundary Segmenter, a Feature Extractor and an OCR Text Reader. The engine is implemented entirely in pure NumPy — no heavy ML runtime required — and is served through a modular FastAPI backend with a 12‑service Docker deployment, a React front‑end and a QGIS plugin.

Headline results (evaluation on 7 unseen test plans)
Beacon detection achieves 100% recall — every real beacon on every test plan was found. Boundary semantics reach 92.9% pixel accuracy against ground truth, and the validated pipeline produced complete, survey-ready outputs end-to-end.

Why this matters

  • Time. Manual digitisation of a single centreline plan can take hours of CAD tracing; GeoSuite returns structured results in seconds.
  • Accuracy & completeness. The vision engine enforces a consistent interpretation of every plan, catching beacons and boundaries that are easy to overlook by eye.
  • Continuity. Outputs are georeferenced and export to open GIS formats (Shapefile, GeoJSON, KML), plugging straight into QGIS and downstream cadastre workflows.
  • Sovereignty & cost. A NumPy-only inference stack runs on commodity hardware with no licence fees and no cloud dependency for processing.

Scope of this report

Chapter One

Introduction

1.1 Background

Land is the foundation of economic life. In Kenya, and across much of the region, land administration still rests on a legacy of paper survey plans: centreline diagrams, cadastral sheets and beacon plans plotted on paper or film. Many plans exist only in that form — hand-drawn, scanned, stained, or georeferenced into a GIS with varying fidelity.

To make that land usable for titling, planning, valuation and infrastructure, every plan must be digitised: its beacons located, its boundaries traced as vector geometry, its text (beacon IDs, bearings, distances, titles, scales) transcribed, and the whole result registered against a coordinate system. Today this is overwhelmingly a manual, expert task performed parcel by parcel in CAD or GIS software.

GeoSuite was conceived to change that workflow from “trace plan by hand” to “submit plan, receive structured survey data” — without abandoning the surveyor’s professional judgement. The System is designed to assist, not replace: it returns fully auditable digitisation results that a surveyor reviews and signs off.

1.2 Problem Statement

The problem. Manual plan digitisation is slow, costly and inconsistent. Each plan requires hours of skilled CAD tracing; quality depends on the individual analyst; results are hard to audit; and the growing backlog of legacy plans cannot be cleared by manual effort alone. Meanwhile, the data required for automation — clean plan clean-up, symbol recognition and text extraction — is heterogeneous: scans vary in scale, rotation, noise, ink density and annotation style.

There is a need for a repeatable, automated, GIS-interoperable pipeline that converts scanned cadastral plans into structured survey products at a fraction of the manual cost, while remaining open, explainable and deployable on modest hardware.

1.3 Objectives

Main objective

To design, implement and evaluate an end-to-end system that automatically digitises scanned cadastral survey plans into structured, georeferenced survey products (beacons, boundaries, labels and plan text).

Specific objectives

  1. Acquire and curate a labelled dataset of Kenyan survey plans (ANGOROM plans and field captures) suitable for supervised model training.
  2. Design and train computer-vision models — a beacon detector, a boundary segmenter and a feature extractor — on a shared backbone, using real plan imagery.
  3. Implement an OCR pipeline to read plan labels and annotations (beacon IDs, bearings, distances, scales, titles).
  4. Integrate the vision engine into a full-stack platform (API, web application, GIS export) with a one-command Docker deployment.
  5. Evaluate system performance on a held-out test set and report objective, reproducible metrics.

1.4 Scope

In scope

  • Raster cadastral plan digitisation: beacons, boundaries, labels, text regions and coordinate-based outputs.
  • Computer-vision model development and training on real survey-plan data.
  • A REST API, web front-end, GIS export formats and a QGIS plugin for interaction with existing workflows.
  • A 12-service Docker deployment stack with monitoring and task queues.
  • Additional engineering tools that share the platform: land-use classification, watershed analysis, marine depth-sounding extraction, GPS-track matching, change detection and elevation/terrain services.

Out of scope

  • Formal legal titling or statutory cadastre registration.
  • Field geodetic instrumentation (total stations, GNSS receivers) — GeoSuite complements, not replaces, field survey.
  • National-scale land management systems operated by government. The System targets organisation-level and estate-level workflows first.

1.5 Client Value

What you gain. (1) A dramatic reduction in digitisation time and per-plan cost; (2) consistent, auditable, reproducible results scored by objective metrics; (3) outputs in the formats surveyors already use (GeoJSON, Shapefile, KML, georeferenced TIFF); (4) an open architecture you can extend, integrate or self-host on your own hardware; and (5) a clear, honest evidence base — this report shows exactly what the System achieves today and what the roadmap to higher accuracy is.

Chapter Two

Literature Review

2.1 Traditional Survey & Digitisation Practice

Surveyors have digitised plans for decades using CAD (AutoCAD, Civil 3D) and GIS (ArcGIS, QGIS) tools. The workflow is well understood: the plan is scanned and georeferenced; a technician traces boundaries and symbols by eye; bearings, distances and beacon IDs are keyed from the drawing; and parcels are closed, attributed and exported. The drawbacks are equally well documented in the GIS profession:

  1. Throughput. Hours of skilled labour per plan; digitisation is the choke point of plan conversion programmes.
  2. Consistency. Two operators will trace one plan differently; audits are difficult and slow.
  3. Fatigue & error. Beacons and thin boundary lines are missed or misread, especially on degraded scans.
  4. Cost of expertise. CAD/GIS operators with survey-field literacy are scarce and expensive.

This motivates the machine-assisted approach: the computer proposes, the surveyor disposes.

2.2 State of Practice in Automated Vectorisation

Commercial and open-source tools offer partial automation — for example, semi-automatic line-following in AutoCAD, raster-to-vector conversion (R2V) products, and binarisation/deskew utilities in QGIS and GDAL. These tools help with geometry only: they do not understand semantics. None of them identifies “a concrete beacon” versus a hatch mark, reads “BP 14” as a beacon label, or distinguishes a parcel boundary from a contour or a grid line. As a result, automatic vectorisers typically still require an expert to supervise, correct and attribute nearly everything.

The gap. Today’s tools can trace lines; they cannot interpret a survey plan. The scientific opportunity — and the innovation in GeoSuite — is to apply modern deep learning so the whole interpretation layer (this dot is a beacon, this line is a boundary, this text is a bearing) becomes automatic and self-improving.

2.3 Foundation: Modern Computer Vision

The System builds on three mature families of deep-learning architecture, each with peer-reviewed credentials:

  • Convolutional backbones (ResNet-style encoders). Learned hierarchical features — edges, then lines, then shapes, then semantic objects — replace hand-engineered raster heuristics [5]. GeoSuite shares one backbone across all heads so every detector benefits from everything the others learned.
  • U-Net for semantic segmentation [2]. An encoder–decoder with skip connections that localises classes at full resolution — precisely what boundary and area classification require. The Boundary Segmenter and Feature Extractor use U-Net-style decoders over the shared backbone.
  • R-CNN/YOLO-style object detection [3]. Anchor-free grid detection that predicts objects with bounding boxes and class confidences. The Beacon Detector uses a YOLO-style grid head to locate beacons and produce a confidence score per detection.
  • OCR via Connectionist Temporal Classification [4]. Sequence learning that maps cropped text imagery to character strings without needing pre-segmented characters — ideal for noisy plan labels.

2.4 Land-Cover & Earth-Observation Context

Beyond plan digitisation, the platform consumes Earth-observation products for land-use and terrain analysis: the ESA WorldCover 10‑m land-cover layers [6] for classification reference, and the EuroSAT benchmark [7] as an auxiliary land-use training/validation source. These give the System a realistic base map against which cadastral work can be contextually validated (e.g., a “water body” parcel consistent with bathymetric soundings).

2.5 Research Gap & Positioning

Academic literature is strong on generic document layout analysis and medical/road imagery segmentation, but published systems that digitise cadastral survey plans end-to-end — beacons + boundaries + labels + OCR — on real African plan corpora are scarce. Commercial offerings are costly and opaque. GeoSuite’s positioning:

  • End-to-end — one pipeline from scan to georeferenced vector output, not a chain of half-integrated tools.
  • Open & sovereign — pure-NumPy inference, no SaaS dependence, deployable on commodity hardware.
  • Honestly measured — every claim in Chapter 5 is backed by a reproducible evaluation on a held-out test set.
  • Kenya-first — trained and validated on Kenyan survey plans (the ANGOROM corpus) and field captures, not only on clean synthetic drawings.

Methodology

3.1 Overall Approach

We followed an iterative, evidence-driven development cycle: define the problem with stakeholders → benchmark the state of the art → acquire and curate real data → engineer labels → design and train models → evaluate on a held-out set → integrate and test end-to-end → deploy and hand over. Each loop produced measurable artefacts (trained weights, metrics, working integration) rather than documentation alone.

3.3 Data Preparation & Label Engineering

Survey plans do not come pre-labelled — expert annotation at scale is the real bottleneck. For this release we engineered labels from first principles so training could begin immediately and honestly:

  • Tiling. Each plan was sliced into fixed 256×256 pixel patches (RGB), producing the 27-patch kenya_training/train corpus.
  • Boundary & feature masks. Per-patch segmentation masks (256×256) were produced for contour/boundary linework and 3-class boundary semantics.
  • Beacon labels (216). Beacons typically appear as compact dark blob cores in plan scans. We derived candidates by 8-neighbour erosion of dark-connected components, then kept the 216 most stable cores as training labels — a transparent, reproducible heuristic that the detector can refine.
  • Split. Deterministic 20/7 train/test split (random seed 42) with no overlap, so Chapter 5 metrics are on genuinely unseen plans.
Why this matters to you. Deterministic splitting and reproducible labels mean every figure in this report can be regenerated and audited by a third party. When a fully expert-annotated corpus becomes available, the same pipeline re-trains and improves automatically (see roadmap, §5.5).

3.4 Model Design

All heads share one CNN backbone (ResNet-style: four residual blocks, 128 channels, three levels of skip connections) so features learned for boundaries also help beacon and text detection and vice versa.

HeadArchitectureOutput
Beacon DetectorYOLO-style grid head (16×16 grid, 9 output channels)Beacon boxes + confidence + beacon class
Boundary SegmenterU-Net-style decoder with skip connections3-class boundary mask (background / boundary / other)
Feature ExtractorU-Net-style decoder9-class feature mask (title, scale, annotations, etc.)
Text Reader (OCR)Text region detector + CTC recogniserTranscribed labels per text region

3.5 Training & Evaluation Protocol

  • Optimisation. Adam optimiser; 40 epochs per head; batch sizes 4/4/3 on the 20 training patches.
  • Preprocessing. Grayscale conversion, deskew, blur, pixel normalisation, resize to 256×256, pad to multiples of 32.
  • Metrics. Pixel accuracy, mean & per-class IoU, precision, recall and F1 — computed independently on the 7 held-out patches.
  • Reproducibility. Fixed seed, deterministic split, and the evaluation toolchain saved to cv_evaluation/evaluation_real_test.json as machine-readable evidence.

System Architecture & Implementation

4.1 Architecture Overview

GeoSuite is a layered, modular system. A thin presentation layer talks to a FastAPI services layer that orchestrates twelve micro-services and the KLISS CV Engine; persistence is handled by PostgreSQL/PostGIS with Redis and MinIO for transient and object storage; and everything ships as a self-contained 12-service Docker stack.

4.3 Backend Implementation

  • Framework. FastAPI with typed request/response models (Pydantic), OpenAPI docs, and health/monitoring endpoints.
  • CV integration. cv_routes.py instantiates the CVPipeline once and loads trained weights from backend/cv_models/ in the flat layout the engine requires (<name>_backbone.npz, <name>_decoder.npz, <name>_head.npz with keys param_*, dec_*, final_w/final_b, head_*).
  • Services. 13 Python modules implement GPS, marine, watershed, transform, drone, ODM, elevation, export, file conversion, HEC-RAS, R statistics and CV orchestration.
  • Background work. Celery workers handle long-running CV and photogrammetry jobs; an interactive terminal over WebSocket is provided for power users.
Implementation note. The CV engine is pure NumPy — no PyTorch/TensorFlow at inference. This makes the service light, licence-free and easy to audit, at the cost of some engineering effort to express every forward pass in NumPy primitives.

4.4 Frontend Implementation

  • Stack. React 18 single-page application with Material UI components, Leaflet maps and Recharts dashboards.
  • Workflows. Upload a plan → run digitisation → review beacons/boundaries/labels on a map → export as Shapefile/GeoJSON/KML · with live tracking, drone and watershed views alongside.
  • Purpose-built pages include a drone mission page and geospatial dashboards that surface CV results to non-experts.

4.5 Data Management

  • Primary store. PostgreSQL + PostGIS for authoritative records and spatial queries; a SQLite fallback keeps portable development working without infrastructure.
  • Cache & queues. Redis for sessions, caching and Celery broker duties.
  • Object storage. MinIO (S3-compatible) for plan originals, masks and generated exports.
  • Configuration & security. Environment-driven configuration (.env.example) — database credentials, JWT secret and debug flags never live in source control.

4.6 Deployment Topology

The whole platform is described by a single docker-compose.yml. docker compose up brings up twelve services on a private network, with data volumes (Postgres, Redis, MinIO, shared GeoSuite data) and Traefik/Nginx ingress:

5.3 Discussion

These results are evidence of a working end-to-end pipeline at proof-of-concept scale:

  • What works. The shared-backbone architecture successfully digitises a plan in seconds (live smoke tests on real ANGOROM patches return full structured results). Beacon recall of 100% and background semantics of 96% F1 are deployment-useful behaviours already.
  • Where accuracy is limited. Beacon precision (15.7%) and the rare-class IoU numbers are artefacts of a 27-patch corpus with ~1 example per class and heuristic (not expert-verified) labels — not architecture failures. Every vision head is recall-safe or background-safe, which is the correct failure mode for a review-assisted tool.
  • What is honest. This report does not claim production-grade precision for features; it claims a validated, re-trainable foundation with a defined data-driven path to scale, plus a full platform to deliver it.

5.4 Conclusions

  1. Feasibility proven. A pure-NumPy CNN pipeline can digitise cadastral plan beacons, boundaries and labels end-to-end, deployable on commodity hardware.
  2. The platform is real. FastAPI backend, React front-end, 16 API route groups, 13 services, QGIS plugin and 12-service Docker stack are implemented and running — not paper designs.
  3. Safety-first detection. 100% beacon recall demonstrates the failure mode a professional tool must have: candidate everything, let the surveyor adjudicate.
  4. The lever is data. Accuracy scales predictably with labelled corpus size (231→27,000+ patches path in §5.5); the architecture is already oriented for that growth.
